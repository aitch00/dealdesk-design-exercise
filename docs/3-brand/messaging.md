# Messaging

> What DealDesk says about itself. The copy is in English, the language of the prototype. The messages are [ASSUMPTION]: written from the brief, not tested with buyers.

## Tagline

**Working choice:** "A second look before it leaves." On the homepage it is the title "A second look before deal messages leave." (UF35): the short form did not say what leaves.

## The one idea of the homepage

**A message can look ready and not be ready.** The homepage tells this one idea (UF45). Its section titles, read in a row, tell the story: "What a second look finds" → "What you fix, and what the deal owner decides" → "Ready means this version" → "What DealDesk doesn't do" → "Take the second look yourself".

## Subtitle

"DealDesk helps boutique transaction-advisory firms check who a deal message is going to and what it promises before it's sent. It asks for a decision only when something needs one." (UF34)

It names the customer in the first screen at every width, and carries the buyer's goal: no slowing of every message.

## Elevator pitch

> DealDesk is a review workspace for boutique transaction-advisory firms. Before a deal message leaves, it checks the recipients against the deal team and flags wording that overstates certainty, right next to the content. Coordinators fix what they can and ask the deal owner for exceptions. Every decision is recorded with the version of the draft it applies to.

## Key messages for the buyer

| # | Message | Proof in the product |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Checks who it's going to.** Recipients are compared with the deal team, not with the client company. | The Sam Lee case |
| 2 | **Flags what it promises.** Wording that states outcomes as certain is highlighted where it appears. | The "guaranteed" sentence |
| 3 | **Exceptions belong to the deal owner.** Coordinators can request them, not approve them. | Owner decisions |
| 4 | **Edits mean a new check.** If a draft changes after review, the old result no longer counts. | The outdated state |
| 5 | **Honest about limits.** Automated checks can miss things. DealDesk supports judgment; it doesn't replace it. | "What DealDesk doesn't do" on the homepage |

## Claims

### Allowed

- "Checks recipients against the deal team before a message leaves."
- "Flags wording that may overstate certainty, next to the sentence itself."
- "If a draft changes after review, DealDesk tells you the check is outdated."
- "Designed to slow you down only when something needs a decision."

### Forbidden [BRIEF + brand]

- Legal, security or compliance guarantees ("secure", "compliant", "guaranteed").
- Invented numbers (losses avoided, % of errors, hours saved).
- Testimonials, client logos, certifications, awards.
- Absolutes: "never", "always", "100%", "every mistake".
- Statistics from data-protection authorities on the homepage: they mix channels and cannot be compared (D9). They belong in the process page, with their limits.
- Published guidance (such as the EDPB guidelines) used as proof that the product works: too close to a compliance claim (UF41).

One exception is checked on purpose: the sample draft on the homepage shows "Completion is guaranteed by 30 June." and "version 2". They appear marked Blocking, as an example of what DealDesk flags, not as a claim (UF40). An automatic test checks the rest of the page for forbidden words and numbers.

## Calls to action

- **Primary:** "Try the sample review". It is the only filled button in the first screen of the homepage (RU4).
- **Secondary:** "Take a second look", under the sample draft on the homepage. It shows the marks next to the lines (2 Blocking, 1 Warning) and goes back (UF46). It is a secondary button, because the main action of the page is to try the sample (RU4).
- "Starting points" in the footer lists every state of the scenario.

The earlier secondary routes, a product image that opened the draft (UF36) and the anchor "See how review works" (UF41), were removed.
